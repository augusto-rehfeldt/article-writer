# Style Guide: Human Academic English

Calibrated on 113 chunks / 730,928 words: 39,263 sentences, mean 18.4 words, SD 17.8, burstiness 0.96. Long sentences (>35 words) 7.4%; short (<10 words) 39.7%. Semicolons 4.92/1k, colons 3.29/1k, parentheses 6.05/1k, em dashes 1.27/1k, brackets 1.22/1k.

## 1. Sentence architecture

Length is bimodal, not average. Nearly 40% of sentences run under ten words and 7% run past thirty-five; the middle is thin. Corpus burstiness sits near 0.96 whole-document, 0.65 median at 1,200-word window. Write both extremes in the same paragraph.

Short: "Money organises every aspect of our lives." / "It is not too late." / "The process takes years." / "Marx had made entirely new and special studies for this part on ground rent, in the seventies." Long, with three or more subordinate hinges: "If the European Civil War is to end with France and Italy abusing their momentary victorious power to destroy Germany and Austria-Hungary now prostrate, they invite their own destruction also, being so deeply and inextricably intertwined with their victims by hidden psychic and economic bonds."

Subordination is left-branching more often than AI's right-branching default. Openers: "Given the inherent multi-disciplinary nature of archaeo-astronomies character…", "Allowing for the above, one may still…", "Looked at from one side, the watt enclosed…", "Whatever human accomplishment we study…", "Notwithstanding their frugal tendencies, many now have little alternative…". Absolute and participial phrases carry weight: "Loaded with cannon and possessed of extraordinary destructive power…", "Seated indeed amid the theatrical trappings of the French Saloons of State, one could wonder…".

Semicolons at 4.92/1k are structural, not ornamental. Three uses appear: joining independent clauses of equal rank ("Freighters came down only eight times a year, unannounced except to syndics actually working at the Port; so when the spectators were lucky enough…"), separating list items already containing commas ("from games to patent medicines, from myths to mass entertainment…"), and marking a turn without a connective ("The book has been written over a number of years; across this time…", "Dystopia, ever the bad conscience of utopia, troubles this fantasy…"). Also: "it also has more contemporary significance"; "Deletion over addition."

Fragments exist but are labelled, not stylistic: figure captions ("Bot: All M strategies."), headings, appositive runs. In running prose, verbless sentences are rare. Do not write "A shift. A rupture. A new order." Nobody here does that.

## 2. Paragraph behaviour

Mean paragraph 190 words, with enormous variance — a 400-word block sits beside a 30-word one, and neither announces the asymmetry. A one-sentence paragraph appears alone: "Why start with money, one might ask?" and "Joyous!"

Openings are frequently *not* topic sentences. They open on procedure ("For the transportation component, we used a simplified version of the popular Four Step Model (4SM) [19]…"), on a concession ("Despite the challenges, there is work that includes and incorporates uncertainty…"), on an example before its category ("England still stands outside Europe."), or mid-argument with a demonstrative pointing back ("This raises a few issues.", "But that is not the worst.", "And then there were the workers."). The thesis, when it arrives, arrives at the end: "In this lies the destructive significance of the Peace of Paris."

Digressions run for a whole paragraph and are not signposted as digressions. Postone's chapter drops into a page on the Grundrisse; Rediker's stops the argument to tell Sarah's story in full; Beer interrupts a 1970 prediction with a bracketed 1980 update ("Here is the second intervention of the new edition. The prediction above is on course."). The return is abrupt: "But let us return to the first edition once more."

## 3. Hedging and stance

Hedges are specific and modal, not decorative. "may be framed as such", "could contribute", "is thought to be important", "seems obviously necessary", "in my judgment", "in my view, the evidence is clear that". Note the pattern: a hedged claim followed by an unhedged one. "There is a heated debate over what caused the extinctions… In my view, the evidence is clear that humans often played a decisive role."

Concession is a full move, not a clause. "We are not offering an unqualified endorsement of embodied cognition; after all it is arguable that even a laptop has a body [16]. However, experimentation has certain physical requirements…" Also: "This might seem fantastical, but a global population of eight billion would have seemed fantastical to a prehistoric hunter-gatherer."

Disagreement names the person and the argument, in that order, with the counter-evidence attached. "One key limitation of these studies is that authentication is mainly performed on hadith texts from literature books. The gold labels for all such texts already exist which questions the utility of such systems." And: "Jafino et al. (2021) go further and argue that an emphasis on the differences across climate scenarios may result in bad adaptation policy advice." Wallace: "there are some important points on which I differ from Mr. Darwin, and I wish to put my opinions on record."

Self-correction is admitted flatly. "How stupid of me not to have thought of that." / "There are two important drawbacks to this approach." / "Unfortunately he was prevented from carrying out this plan." Limits are stated as facts, not apologies: "the uncertainty in the tropics is large reflecting the sparse data there", "The actual width of the transition has not been estimated."

## 4. Punctuation and typography

Parentheses (6.05/1k) outnumber every other mark and do four jobs: citation, gloss, aside, and worked example. "(Kitchen, 2020; Turner, Duggan, Glezerson, & Marshall, 2020)"; "work experience (measured as age minus years of education minus six) and its square"; "(Certainly, there is no need to calculate the sum for s = v.3 in rows 2, 4, and 6…)"; "(i.e., appear insane or mindless)". A parenthesis holding a full sentence with its own terminal punctuation is normal.

Em dashes are rare — 1.27/1k, roughly one per 800 words. They mark interruption or a final swerve: "the only boundary wall on their world", "a framework that, once learned, allows any properly trained human to read the alien symbols", "the finance industry commandeers our elected governments". Never use one where a comma works.

Colons (3.29/1k) introduce lists, definitions and block quotations: "Five key aspects of CC systems. These include a) flexibility of knowledge representations, b) tolerance to ambiguity…" Lettered and numbered enumerations inside a sentence are common.

Quotation: double quotes for verbatim source ("policy paralysis in response to what is a highly uncertain phenomena..."), and scare quotes for a term held at arm's length — "reform" of 1861, "human capital", "low skill", "hybrid" approach, "gypsy merchants". The ellipsis inside a quotation preserves the source's own trailing dots.

Citations are field-specific and mixed across the corpus — author-date (Bell et al. 2017), author-date-with-ampersand (Falomir & Olteţeanu, 2019), numeric brackets [29], and named-numeric [19]. Pick one and hold it. Page-precise quotation carries a locator; paraphrase does not.

## 5. Argumentative moves

Sources are introduced by name and *what they did*, then contrasted with an explicit hinge. "Like many other studies, Das and Hilgenstock (2018) use a Routine Task Intensity (RTI) measure constructed by Autor and Dorn (2013)…" then "There are two important drawbacks to this approach. First… Lewandowski et al., (2019) compare task intensity measures… and find sizable cross-country differences." The hinge words are plain: "In contrast to reasoning about objects…", "In similar work…", "By contrast, true automation takes place…", "Contrariwise…".

Rhetorical questions are real questions the text then answers. "Have the automation theorists got this story right?" / "Why start with money, one might ask?" / "How many people in the future will be alive at any one time?" / "What is the use of the ever-faster, ever-slicker, more nearly perfect implementation of rotten plans?" One or two per section, never in a row.

First person is used for method and for judgment, both. "We conducted a narrative, structured review…"; "We began by dividing Copenhagen…"; "I rely on the following definitions"; "It is my hope that readers will find this theoretical approach useful"; "I want us to understand Utopianism, not as…". Both "we" (agentive, plural) and "I" (evaluative, singular) appear.

Numbers are exact and carry their uncertainty. "9% K −1 (90% confidence interval 6-14% K −1)"; "5.2 percent per year, on average, between 1950 and 1973"; "Researchers from twenty three projects responded to the survey and twelve did not, a 66% response rate." Spelled-out and numeric forms coexist within a paragraph.

## 6. Vocabulary

TTR 0.0499 overall, 0.41 median per window — moderate, not lexically showy. Field jargon is used unglossed once established, and *defined on first use with a formula* where it matters: "the rate of growth of output (ΔO) minus the rate of growth of labor productivity (ΔP) equals the rate of growth of employment (ΔE)."

Abstraction is anchored by concrete particulars in the same paragraph. Not "technological change reshapes labour" but "coin + pliers = screwdriver"; "using a pan as a hammer"; "assembling matchstick boxes for pennies a pop"; "sellers of vegetables, chestnuts and shoe laces; purveyors of pizzas, mussels, recycled clothes". Keynes reaches for "the tragi-comic masks of some strange drama or puppet-show".

Idiomatic texture is dry and occasional: "without teeth", "with no quickset of pedantry between the author and the reader", "a lift into the blue", "a parade of landmark research". One per few pages, never clustered.

The corpus's own high-frequency scaffolding — "the fact that", "at the same time", "on the other hand", "point of view", "that is to say", "in connection with", "as regards the" — is *permitted*. These read as human because human academics genuinely overuse them.

## 7. What none of these writers do

- **Tricolons.** No "not X, not Y, but Z". No three-item parallel escalation as a rhythm device.
- **Antithesis as default.** "no es X sino Y" / "not merely X but Y" appears, but rarely — never more than once per long section.
- **Verdict paragraphs.** No short standalone paragraph delivering a judgment: "The question is not technological." Nobody writes this.
- **Metadiscursive connectives.** No "let us begin by", "let us turn to", "it is worth noting that", "importantly", "crucially", "notably" as paragraph-openers. Transitions are content, not signposting.
- **Stock LLM phrasing.** No "delve into", "landscape of", "sheds light on", "raises the question", "underscores", "at its core", "navigate the complexities", "a testament to", "plays a pivotal role", "it is important to note".
- **Symmetrical paragraphs.** No three paragraphs of similar length, each opening on a topic sentence and closing on a summary.
- **Balanced closings.** No section ending on a synthesis that restates the section. Sections end on a limitation, a number, a citation, or mid-thought.
- **Hedge stacking.** No "may potentially somewhat suggest". One hedge per claim.
- **Programme endings.** No "future work should therefore explore…" as a closing flourish. Open questions are named specifically or not at all.
- **Uniform citation density.** Some paragraphs carry six citations, some carry none. AI text spreads them evenly.
- **Enthusiasm about the subject.** No "fascinating", "remarkable", "profound implications". The strongest available adjective is "interesting", and it is used sparingly: "The latter is particularly interesting, because…".
